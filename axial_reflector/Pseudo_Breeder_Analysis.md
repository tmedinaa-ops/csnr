# Pseudo-breeder thought experiment: can we breed plutonium to slow the reactivity loss

The instinct is good and it aims at the right mechanism. The depletion showed the reactivity slope is steep partly because this HEU core breeds almost nothing, about 4 grams of plutonium over 7 years, so burnup turns almost directly into reactivity loss with nothing regenerated. Adding fertile material to breed fissile in place would, in principle, refill some of what you burn and flatten the slope. That attacks the loss rate itself, which reflection does not. So the idea is pointed at the real problem.

The trouble is that SNAP's neutron economy cannot afford it as it stands, and the reason is the same leakage-bound condition that motivated the reflector.

## Why the current core breeds nothing

Breeding needs spare neutrons. Every fission from U-235 releases about 2.4 neutrons; one must go on to sustain the chain, and what is left over after leakage and parasitic absorption is what you have to breed with. The relevant number is eta, neutrons produced per neutron absorbed in the fuel. For U-235 in a thermal-to-epithermal spectrum eta is about 2.07. One of those sustains the chain, leaving roughly 0.07 per absorption, and on this core a quarter to a third of neutrons leak before they can do anything. There is essentially no surplus, which is exactly why the measured conversion ratio is about 0.014 (4 g bred against 281 g burned). The core is not failing to breed by accident; it has no neutrons to spare.

## Adding fertile material in-core makes criticality worse now

Here is the tension. A fertile isotope, U-238 or Th-232, is a neutron absorber. Putting it in the core to breed means those captures compete with the fuel for a neutron budget that is already tight. k-inf is only about 1.4, and with the leakage penalty k-eff is barely at 1.0. Add enough fertile material to breed meaningfully and k-inf falls below the 1/P_NL you need to stay critical, and the reactor goes subcritical. So you can only add a little in-core fertile before you lose criticality, which caps the breeding at a level too small to matter. You would be paying reactivity now for a small payback later, on a core that has no reactivity to pay with.

Spectrum makes it worse for the obvious choice. U-238 to Pu-239 breeds well in a fast spectrum, where eta is higher and U-238 fast fission helps. SNAP is the opposite, a soft hydride-moderated spectrum, which is the worst case for U-238 breeding. Thorium to U-233 is the better pair for a soft spectrum, because U-233 is the one fissile whose eta stays above about 2.2 from thermal through epithermal, so it is the only one that can breed without a fast spectrum. But thorium brings its own costs: the intermediate Pa-233 has a 27-day half-life, so the bred U-233 shows up weeks after the capture and the reactivity credit is delayed, and you are now producing U-233, which carries handling and safeguards weight the project would have to justify.

## The version that respects the neutron economy: a fertile blanket on the leakage

There is one place where breeding does not steal neutrons from the critical core, and it is the same place the reflector goes. The neutrons that leak out the ends and sides are already lost. Put fertile material where they leave, in the reflector or end region, and they breed on their way out instead of vanishing. A depleted-uranium or thorium blanket does double duty, it reflects some neutrons back like beryllium and captures others to breed, and it does not rob the chain because those neutrons were leaving anyway. This is the classic breeder-blanket idea, and it is the only form of the pseudo-breeder that is neutronically honest on a core this tight.

Even the blanket has hard limits here. The fissile it breeds sits in the blanket, at the periphery where neutron importance is low, so its reactivity worth per gram is a fraction of fuel in the core center; it slows the reactivity loss modestly rather than regenerating the core. You cannot reprocess it in space, so it only ever helps as in-situ reactivity, not as recovered fuel. And it is heavy. Depleted uranium is about 19 g/cc, thorium about 11.7; a blanket thick enough to breed usefully adds tens of kilograms, which on a space reactor competes directly with the radiator and convertor mass and drives specific power the wrong way. That mass penalty is usually what kills fertile blankets on space reactors and keeps beryllium, at 1.85 g/cc, as the reflector of choice.

## Where this lands

As a bolt-on to SNAP, a pseudo-breeder is a mitigation at best, not a cure. Realistically it could shave the reactivity slope by some tens of percent if you got a blanket to work, not eliminate it, and it costs mass and complexity to do so. The reason is structural: a real breeder needs a fast spectrum and a fertile-heavy core, which is a fundamentally different reactor, the liquid-metal fast-reactor or space-fast-reactor lineage, not a hydride-moderated SNAP. That is the same "this is a different reactor" boundary the project keeps running into with more pins, bigger cores, and refractory clad.

So the clean-hands answer is to reflect first. Reflection and breeding are competing claims on the same leaking neutrons, and reflection is the cheaper, lighter, higher-yield claim: it recovers those neutrons as immediate margin at 1.85 g/cc, where breeding recovers a fraction of them as delayed, low-importance, heavy fissile. If reflection alone does not buy enough horizon and the mass budget genuinely has room, a fertile blanket in the reflector region is the next thing to model, U-238 if the spectrum is hardened at all, thorium if it stays soft. But it earns its place only after the reflector has taken the easy neutrons, and only if `leakage_split.py` shows there are enough leaking neutrons to be worth breeding on.

## If you want to test it anyway

The experiment is cheap to set up once the reflector variant exists, because it is the same geometry with a different blanket material. Replace the beryllium end (or radial) reflector in the variant with a fertile blanket, depleted uranium or thorium metal, and deplete it. Watch three things: the immediate Δk when you swap beryllium for fertile (it will drop, that is the parasitic cost), the plutonium or U-233 built in the blanket over the mission, and whether the reactivity slope flattens enough to justify the k you gave up and the mass you added. My expectation is that beryllium wins on horizon per kilogram at these dimensions, but the run makes it a number instead of an argument, and a beryllium-then-thin-fertile hybrid is worth trying since the reflector recovers the margin and a modest fertile layer behind it breeds on what still leaks.
